2.1. Thrasyllus of Mendes: The Performance of Terror
The archetype of the "Survivalist Astrologer" is Thrasyllus of Mendes, the personal astrologer to the Roman Emperor Tiberius (reigned 14–37 CE). Tiberius was a paranoid tyrant who famously purged his court of potential rivals and employed astrologers to identify threats. However, Tiberius was also deeply suspicious of the astrologers themselves, fearing they might manipulate him.
The Case:
According to the historian Tacitus in his Annals (Book VI), Tiberius developed a brutal test for his astrologers at his cliffside villa on Capri. He would ask them to cast his horoscope and predict the future. If the astrologer delivered a flattering or generic prediction, Tiberius—suspecting flattery—would have the astrologer thrown from the cliff into the sea by his guards.
When Thrasyllus was subjected to this test, he cast the chart for the moment. However, he realized the trap.
The Error (Theatrical Manipulation): Thrasyllus did not provide an objective reading of the Emperor’s chart. Instead, he calculated the positions for the current moment (a "decumbiture" or interrogation chart) and feigned extreme terror. Tacitus records that Thrasyllus "appeared to tremble" and, as he calculated the aspects, exclaimed that he was in a "critical hour" and "near death".1 This was a masterful lie. By predicting his own imminent danger, he validated Tiberius's secret intent to kill him. Tiberius, impressed that the stars had revealed his own private thoughts to the astrologer, spared Thrasyllus and retained him as his most trusted advisor.
The Audit:
This is the Court Flattery Error in its most extreme form. Thrasyllus’s output was not derived from a detached analysis of celestial mechanics; it was a psychological counter-move designed to save his life. The data was weaponized for survival. The Case:
In 1556, shortly after the publication of Les Prophéties, Catherine summoned Nostradamus to the court to draw the horoscopes of the young Valois princes.
The Error (Protective Omission): The astrological data for the Valois children was catastrophic. For instance, he famously predicted the death of her husband Henri II in a joust (Century I, 35: "The young lion will overcome the old one"), but this was wrapped in such obscurity that it was only recognized post-facto. Regarding the sons, he avoided direct predictions of "dynastic extinction," choosing instead to secure his funding and status by maintaining ambiguity.

The Error (Political Bias): The appearance of the Comet of 1577 caused panic in the court. A destabilized court was dangerous for the Queen and for Dee’s own position. The Case:
Lilly published annual almanacs (Merlinus Anglicus) that were distributed to troops to boost morale. In one famous instance, he was asked to predict the outcome of the Parliamentarian military campaigns.
The Error (The "Safe" Bet): Lilly predicted that victory or significant events would occur in the "West." Later, in his own autobiography, he admitted the cynicism of this prediction. Lilly essentially hedged his bet. He used a loose astrological association (Gemini = West) to align with a likely military outcome that would please his patrons.
