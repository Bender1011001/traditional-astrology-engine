## Historical Foundations of Temporal Hours and Chaldean Order

### Temporal Hours: An Astronomical Tradition

The concept of dividing day and night into twelve equal parts each—regardless of seasonal variations—forms the fundamental basis of the unequal hours system. This temporal hour tradition, also known as seasonal hours, biblical hours, or Roman hours (*horae temporales*), has its origins in the ancient observation that days and nights vary in length throughout the year depending on latitude and season[23]. The astronomical basis for this system corresponds to the "day arc of the Sun" concept, where daylight duration varies with latitude and season—ranging from 16 equinoctial hours in summer to 8 equinoctial hours in winter at approximately 49° north/south latitude[23]. 

The first documentation of dividing day and night into twelve hours each appears in Ancient Egyptian records, with similar divisions later adopted throughout the Mediterranean basin during Classical Greek Antiquity[23]. This system was deeply integrated into daily life and religious observance, particularly influencing the monastic "Prayer of the Hours" tradition where daily activities were scheduled according to the temporal hours rather than fixed clock time[23]. 

Vettius Valens, the influential 2nd century Hellenistic astrologer, provides crucial historical documentation of this system in his Anthologies. His work demonstrates the sophisticated integration of planetary hours into astrological practice, where the system was used not only for timing but also for deeper cosmological understanding[11]. Valens' description of planetary hours reveals the methodical calculation process used in antiquity, where the day was divided into 24 hours based on the actual solar cycle rather than fixed clock time[11]. 

Of particular importance in Valens' work is the fractional approach to hour calculation, where he notes: "the hour I of night [is that] of Mercury; the second, [that] of [the] Moon; the third, [that] of Saturn; the fourth, [that] of Jupiter; the fifth, [that] of Mars; the sixth, [that] of [the] Sun; the seventh, [that] of Venus"[11]. This fractional division—particularly the detailed description of hour calculations—demonstrates the precision with which ancient astrologers approached temporal hour computation.

### Chaldean Order: The Sequential Foundation

The planetary sequence employed in the planetary hours system follows what became known as the "Chaldean order"—Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, and Moon—repeating endlessly[1]. This sequence is not arbitrary but reflects the relative orbital velocity of the planets as observed from Earth, with Saturn appearing to move most slowly and the Moon appearing to move most rapidly[1]. 

From a heliocentric perspective, this sequence also indicates the relative distance of the planets from the center of their orbits, with the Sun taking the Earth's position in the sequence[1]. The traditional geocentric perspective interprets this arrangement as the structure of the planetary spheres—the conceptual model of nested celestial spheres that dominated ancient cosmology[1].

William Lilly, the 17th century English astrologer, provides authoritative documentation of this system in his seminal work *Christian Astrology* (1647). Lilly explicitly states: "You must understand that as there are seven days of the week, viz. Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday, Saturday, so there are seven Planets, viz. Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sol, Venus, Mercury, Luna"[7]. This sequence forms the critical foundation for both planetary day and hour calculations.

The mathematical principle underlying the planetary days system reveals why our modern weekdays follow this specific sequence. As Lilly explains, "the planet that rules the first hour of the day is also the ruler of the whole day and gives the day its name. Thus the first hour of Sunday is ruled by the Sun, the first hour of Monday is ruled by the Moon and so on"[1]. This creates the familiar weekday sequence where each day's name corresponds to the planet ruling its first hour.

The astronomical explanation for this sequence is mathematically precise: with 24 hours divided among seven planets, each day's first hour begins with the planet three positions down the Chaldean order from the previous day's starting planet (calculated as 24 ÷ 7 = 3 remainder 3)[2]. Starting with the Sun for Sunday, the sequence naturally progresses through Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn before returning to the Sun for the following Sunday[2].

### Comparison of Historical Day-Start Conventions

Historical sources reveal a significant divergence in how the day was defined for planetary hour calculations. Two primary conventions emerged:

1. **Sunset convention**: Used by Babylonians, Greeks, and early Hellenistic astrologers including Vettius Valens. According to Valens, "the first hour of the day began at sunset, which follows Greek and Babylonian convention"[2]. This is confirmed by historical evidence, such as a Pompeian graffito dated to February 6, 60 CE that identifies the date as "Sunday," matching the sunset convention even though modern reckoning would consider it a Wednesday[2].

2. **Sunrise convention**: Adopted by Egyptians and later becoming the predominant method in Medieval and Renaissance astrology. This convention begins the planetary day at sunrise, aligning with the natural progression of daylight[8]. William Lilly's *Christian Astrology* reflects this sunrise convention, dividing the day into "the period that extends from sunrise to sunset (daylight) [and] the period extending from sunset to sunrise of the next day (nighttime)"[1].

The Egyptian sunrise convention ultimately prevailed in Western astrological tradition, as evidenced by the enduring phrase "the Sun rises" rather than "the horizon descends." This convention also aligns with natural human experience—the transition from night to day begins with the first light of sunrise, not the end of darkness at sunset.

For our implementation, we adopt the sunrise convention as the default standard. This choice aligns with both the predominant modern astrological practice and the rationale that human activity naturally begins with daylight. The sunrise convention also creates a more intuitive progression: each day begins with light rather than darkness, and the planetary day corresponds roughly with the civil day (dawn to dawn rather than dusk to dusk).

## Core Algorithm for Planetary Hour Calculation

### Temporal Hour Computation with Precise Boundaries

The fundamental operation in implementing planetary hours involves dividing the solar cycle into 24 unequal hours—12 for daylight (sunrise to sunset) and 12 for nighttime (sunset to next sunrise). Unlike our modern equal hours (always 60 minutes), these temporal hours vary in duration throughout the year depending on the local daylight/night cycle.

Following authoritative sources including Vettius Valens and William Lilly, we define:

1. **Day length** = sunset - sunrise (time duration)
2. **Day-hour length** = day length / 12 (each of the 12 daytime hours)
3. **Night length** = next sunrise - sunset (time duration)
4. **Night-hour length** = night length / 12 (each of the 12 nighttime hours)

The critical precision required for implementation involves the exact determination of which hour applies at the precise moment of sunrise and sunset. Historical sources are not explicit about these boundaries, so we must derive logical consistent rules based on the fundamental principles.

#### Sunrise and Sunset Definition Standards

For topocentric calculations, we adopt the standard astronomical definition used in modern planetary hour calculators:

- **Sunrise** is defined as the instant when the upper limb of the Sun appears on the true horizon with standard atmospheric refraction[15]. This occurs when the Sun's center is approximately 50 arcminutes (about 0.83°) below the true horizon[15].
- **Sunset** follows the same definition, occurring when the upper limb of the Sun disappears below the true horizon with standard refraction[15].

This definition accounts for both the Sun's apparent diameter (about 0.5°) and atmospheric refraction (about 0.58°), which together explain why the upper limb appears at the horizon when the center is still below it[15]. Civil twilight occurs when the Sun's center is 6° below the horizon, nautical twilight at 12°, and astronomical twilight at 18°[15].

Unlike some alternative systems that might use civil twilight as the day/night boundary, we strictly adhere to the upper limb definition because traditional planetary hours literature explicitly references sunrise and sunset—not twilight—as the day/night transition points[1][7]. The definitive work of Vettius Valens, for example, calculates hours based on the actual solar cycle without reference to twilight periods[11].

#### Temporal Hour Boundaries with Inequality Rules

To establish precise computational rules, we must define the exact boundaries between hours, especially at sunrise and sunset:

- **Day hours** (numbered 1-12): Begin at sunrise and end at sunset
- **Night hours** (numbered 1-12): Begin at sunset and end at next sunrise

With precise inequality definitions:

1. **Day hours**:
   - Hour 1 (first day hour): sunrise ≤ time < sunrise + day-hour length
   - Hour n (2-12): sunrise + (n-1) × day-hour length ≤ time < sunrise + n × day-hour length
   - Last day hour ends at: sunset (exclusive upper bound)

2. **Night hours**:
   - Hour 1 (first night hour): sunset ≤ time < sunset + night-hour length
   - Hour n (2-12): sunset + (n-1) × night-hour length ≤ time < sunset + n × night-hour length
   - Last night hour ends at: next sunrise (exclusive upper bound)

This creates the following precise boundary conditions:
- At the exact moment of sunrise: Day Hour 1 begins (inclusive lower bound)
- At the exact moment of sunset: Night Hour 1 begins (inclusive lower bound)
- One second before sunrise: Previous day's Night Hour 12 (exclusive upper bound)
- One second before sunset: Current day's Day Hour 12 (exclusive upper bound)

The choice of inclusive lower bound at sunrise/sunset follows logically from the fundamental definition of the planetary day beginning at sunrise[1]. Since sunrise marks the start of the new planetary day, that instant properly belongs to the new day's first hour rather than the previous night's last hour.

This boundary definition also creates a mathematically consistent system where every moment in time is assigned precisely one planetary hour, with no gaps or overlaps. The computational advantage is significant—a simple "greater than or equal to" test determines whether a timestamp belongs to the current day or night cycle.

### Planetary Day Ruler Determination

The planetary day ruler follows the traditional sequence where each day is named for the planet ruling its first hour:

- Sunday: Sun
- Monday: Moon
- Tuesday: Mars
- Wednesday: Mercury
- Thursday: Jupiter
- Friday: Venus
- Saturday: Saturn

This weekday sequence derives directly from the planetary hours system, not the reverse. As William Lilly states in *Christian Astrology*: "the planet that rules the first hour of the day is also the ruler of the whole day and gives the day its name. Thus the first hour of Sunday is ruled by the Sun, the first hour of Monday is ruled by the Moon and so on"[1].

The underlying astronomical principle is precise and mathematical. With 24 hours divided among 7 planets, each day's first hour begins with the planet three positions down the Chaldean order from the previous day's starting planet (24 ÷ 7 = 3 remainder 3)[2]. This creates the unbroken cycle that has persisted essentially unchanged since antiquity:

- Sun → Moon → Mars → Mercury → Jupiter → Venus → Saturn → Sun...

This sequence has remarkable historical continuity. As noted in planetary hours literature, "we would note that from the introduction of the week in the classical period that the order has never varied or been interrupted. For example the adoption of the Gregorian calendar changed the date, but not the endless cycle of the days of the week"[1].

The planetary day ruler is determined by the civil calendar day but with crucial understanding: the planetary day begins at sunrise, not midnight. This means 3:00 AM on a Tuesday morning (civil calendar) actually belongs to Monday's planetary day (from sunset Monday to sunrise Tuesday)[8]. This distinction is critical for accurate implementation.

To determine the planetary day ruler algorithmically:
1. Identify the civil calendar date at the timestamp
2. Determine if the timestamp is before or after the day's sunrise
3. If before sunrise: use previous civil date's planetary ruler
4. If after sunrise: use current civil date's planetary ruler

This accounts for the fundamental reality that the planetary day begins at sunrise, not midnight, creating a ~12 hour offset between civil calendar days and planetary days during nighttime hours.

### Planetary Hour Ruler Assignment Sequence

The planetary hour ruler sequence follows the Chaldean order: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon, repeating continuously[1][2]. This is the sequence from slowest to fastest moving planets as observed from Earth, or from farthest to nearest in the traditional planetary spheres model.

The hour ruler assignment follows precise rules:
1. The first hour of the day (at sunrise) is ruled by the day's planetary ruler
2. Each subsequent hour advances to the next planet in Chaldean order
3. After the Moon comes Saturn, continuing the cycle
4. Day and night hours follow the same sequence without interruption

For example, on Sunday:
- Day Hour 1: Sun (rules Sunday)
- Day Hour 2: Venus (next in Chaldean order after Sun)
- Day Hour 3: Mercury
- Day Hour 4: Moon
- Day Hour 5: Saturn
- Day Hour 6: Jupiter
- Day Hour 7: Mars
- Day Hour 8: Sun
- Day Hour 9: Venus
- Day Hour 10: Mercury
- Day Hour 11: Moon
- Day Hour 12: Saturn
- Night Hour 1: Jupiter
- Night Hour 2: Mars
- Night Hour 3: Sun
... and so on until the next sunrise

The progression through Chaldean order continues seamlessly from day to night hours. This creates the critical situation where the planet ruling the first night hour (at sunset) is precisely three positions ahead of the day's ruler in the Chaldean sequence (Sun → Venus → Mercury → Moon for Sunday night)[1].

This progression is mathematically consistent and can be verified by noting that the 24th hour of Sunday (the last night hour) is ruled by Mercury. The next planet in Chaldean order is the Moon, which properly begins Monday's first hour[1].

Modern sunrise/sunset calculation services often provide times in local standard time with an optional DST adjustment. ## Edge Case Handling Protocol

### Exact Moment of Sunrise and Sunset

The precise moments of sunrise and sunset present the most critical boundary conditions for the algorithm. Historical sources don't explicitly define which hour applies at these exact instants, so we must apply logical principles consistent with the system's fundamental structure.

**At exact sunrise**:
- Sunrise marks the beginning of a new planetary day[1]
- This instant properly belongs to the new day's first hour
- Implementation: sunrise timestamp is included in Day Hour 1 (inclusive lower bound)

**At exact sunset**:
- Sunset marks the transition from day to night hours
- This instant properly belongs to the night hours period
- Implementation: sunset timestamp is included in Night Hour 1 (inclusive lower bound)

This boundary definition creates a mathematically consistent system where every instantaneous moment belongs to exactly one hour with no gaps or overlaps. The rationale is based on the fundamental principle that sunrise begins the day and sunset begins the night—they don't mark the end of the previous period.

This approach is consistent with William Lilly's description of the temporal hour system where "the period that extends from sunrise to sunset (daylight) is divided into twelve hours and the period extending from sunset to sunrise of the next day (nighttime) is also divided into twelve hours"[1].

### Midnight Transition Across Planetary Days

The transition through midnight presents a subtle but important case because the civil calendar date changes, but the planetary day does not. Midnight typically occurs during the night hours of the current planetary day (which began at the previous sunrise).

For example, on Tuesday night (civil calendar):
- From civil Tuesday sunrise to civil Wednesday sunrise = Tuesday planetary day
- Midnight occurs between civil Tuesday sunset and civil Wednesday sunrise
- This midnight period belongs to Tuesday's planetary day (night hours)

Therefore:
- All timestamps from civil Monday sunrise to civil Tuesday sunrise = Monday planetary day
- All timestamps from civil Tuesday sunrise to civil Wednesday sunrise = Tuesday planetary day
- Midnight between civil Monday and Tuesday occurs during Monday's planetary day (night hours)

The algorithm handles this through the fundamental rule: the planetary day begins at sunrise, not midnight. Timestamps are evaluated against the sunrise/sunset boundaries rather than civil date boundaries.

The computational approach is:
1. Determine current civil date at timestamp
2. Find sunrise for current civil date
3. If timestamp < sunrise: belongs to previous civil date's planetary day
4. If timestamp ≥ sunrise: belongs to current civil date's planetary day

This correctly handles the midnight transition and ensures all timestamps properly associate with their planetary day regardless of civil calendar changes.

### High-Latitude and Polar Region Adaptation

The standard planetary hours calculation faces significant challenges at high latitudes where daylight patterns become extreme:

1. **Polar day** (midnight sun): Sun remains above horizon for ≥24 hours
2. **Polar night**: Sun remains below horizon for ≥24 hours
3. **Very short day/night**: Extreme seasonal variations where one period is significantly shorter than the other

The astronomical reality is that above the polar circles (approximately 66.5° latitude), the sun does not set every day in summer or rise every day in winter[16][23]. At the poles, polar day and polar night each last approximately 179 days (north pole) or 186 days (south pole)[16].

#### Polar Day Handling (24+ hours of daylight)

When the Sun never sets (polar day):
- Day length = 24+ hours (exactly)
- Night length = 0 hours (no sunset)
- Standard calculation fails because night length = 0

Recommended implementation policy:
- If sunrise < previous sunrise (indicating continuous daylight):
  - Treat as continuous daylight cycle
  - Use equal hours (60 minutes) for all 24 hours
  - Apply planetary hours sequence normally, starting with day's ruling planet at civil midnight
  - Add warning: "Polar day conditions - using equal hours approximation"

This approach is a modern engineering adaptation since historical sources didn't address polar regions. The equal hours approximation maintains functionality while acknowledging the physical impossibility of dividing a 0-length night period.

#### Polar Night Handling (24+ hours of darkness)

When the Sun never rises (polar night):
- Day length = 0 hours (no sunrise)
- Night length = 24+ hours (exactly)
- Standard calculation fails because day length = 0

Recommended implementation policy:
- If sunset > previous sunset (indicating continuous darkness):
  - Treat as continuous night cycle
  - Use equal hours (60 minutes) for all 24 hours
  - Apply planetary hours sequence normally, starting with night's ruling planet at civil midnight
  - Add warning: "Polar night conditions - using equal hours approximation"

#### Very Short Day/Night Handling

When day or night length is extremely short (e.g., < 30 minutes):
- Creates temporally compressed hours that may be impractical
- Could result in hours of just a few minutes duration

Recommended implementation policy:
- If day length < threshold (e.g., 60 minutes):
  - Calculate day hours normally but flag extremely short hours
  - Continue normal night hour calculation
- If night length < threshold (e.g., 60 minutes):
  - Calculate night hours normally but flag extremely short hours
  - Continue normal day hour calculation

This preserves the traditional calculation method while providing warnings about potentially problematic results. Historical sources don't address these extreme cases, so this represents a prudent modern adaptation.

```python
def compute_temporal_hours(sunrise, sunset, next_sunrise):
    """
    Compute temporal hour lengths and boundaries.
    
    Parameters:
        sunrise: datetime (local standard time, topocentric apparent)
        sunset: datetime (local standard time, topocentric apparent)
        next_sunrise: datetime (local standard time, topocentric apparent)
        
    Returns:
        dict containing:
        - day_hour_length: timedelta
        - night_hour_length: timedelta
        - day_hours: list of (start, end, hour_index) tuples for day hours
        - night_hours: list of (start, end, hour_index) tuples for night hours
        - is_polar_day: boolean
        - is_polar_night: boolean
    """
    # Check for polar day/night conditions
    is_polar_day = sunset > next_sunrise  # Continuous daylight
    is_polar_night = sunrise > sunset     # Continuous darkness
    
    # Polar day conditions
    if is_polar_day:
        # Calculate using 24 equal hours
        day_hour_length = timedelta(hours=2)
        night_hour_length = day_hour_length
        day_start = sunrise
        day_hours = []
        night_hours = []
        
        # Create 24 equal hours
        for i in range(24):
            start = day_start + (i * day_hour_length)
            end = start + day_hour_length
            # For polar day, everything is 'day' conceptually
            day_hours.append((start, end, i+1))
            
        return {
            "day_hour_length": day_hour_length,
            "night_hour_length": night_hour_length,
            "day_hours": day_hours,
            "night_hours": [],
            "is_polar_day": True,
            "is_polar_night": False
        }
    
    # Polar night conditions
    if is_polar_night:
        # Calculate using 24 equal hours
        night_hour_length = timedelta(hours=2)
        day_hour_length = night_hour_length
        night_start = sunrise  # midnight conceptually
        day_hours = []
        night_hours = []
        
        # Create 24 equal hours
        for i in range(24):
            start = night_start + (i * night_hour_length)
            end = start + night_hour_length
            # For polar night, everything is 'night' conceptually
            night_hours.append((start, end, i+1))
            
        return {
            "day_hour_length": day_hour_length,
            "night_hour_length": night_hour_length,
            "day_hours": [],
            "night_hours": night_hours,
            "is_polar_day": False,
            "is_polar_night": True
        }
    
    # Normal conditions - calculate actual temporal hours
    day_length = sunset - sunrise
    day_hour_length = day_length / 12
    
    night_length = next_sunrise - sunset
    night_hour_length = night_length / 12
    
    # Calculate day hour boundaries
    day_hours = []
    for i in range(12):
        start = sunrise + (i * day_hour_length)
        end = start + day_hour_length
        day_hours.append((start, end, i+1))
    
    # Calculate night hour boundaries
    night_hours = []
    for i in range(12):
        start = sunset + (i * night_hour_length)
        end = start + night_hour_length
        night_hours.append((start, end, i+1))
    
    return {
        "day_hour_length": day_hour_length,
        "night_hour_length": night_hour_length,
        "day_hours": day_hours,
        "night_hours": night_hours,
        "is_polar_day": False,
        "is_polar_night": False
    }

def planetary_day_ruler(date, sunrise, sunset, next_sunrise, current_datetime):
    """
    Determine the planetary ruler for the current planetary day.
    
    Parameters:
        date: datetime (civil calendar date)
        sunrise: datetime (local standard time, topocentric apparent)
        sunset: datetime (local standard time, topocentric apparent)
        next_sunrise: datetime (local standard time, topocentric apparent)
        current_datetime: datetime (timestamp to evaluate)
        
    Returns:
        string: planetary ruler for the current planetary day
    """
    # Determine if timestamp occurs before or after sunrise
    if current_datetime < sunrise:
        # Timestamp is before today's sunrise, so it's part of yesterday's planetary day
        planetary_date = date - timedelta(days=1)
    else:
        # Timestamp is after today's sunrise, so it's part of today's planetary day
        planetary_date = date
    
    # Get day of week (0=Monday, 6=Sunday)
    weekday = planetary_date.weekday()
    
    # Planetary day rulers (Monday=0 through Sunday=6)
    planetary_rulers = [
        "Moon",      # Monday
        "Mars",      # Tuesday
        "Mercury",   # Wednesday
        "Jupiter",   # Thursday
        "Venus",     # Friday
        "Saturn",    # Saturday
        "Sun"        # Sunday
    ]
    
    return planetary_rulers[weekday]

def planetary_hour_ruler(datetime, sunrise, sunset, next_sunrise, timezone):

    # Initialize result
    result = {

        # Using equal hours for polar day
        hours_since_midnight = (datetime - datetime.replace(hour=0, minute=0, second=0, microsecond=0)).total_seconds() / 3600.0
        hour_index = int((hours_since_midnight % 24) / 2) + 1
        
        # Determine planet (starting with Sun at midnight)
        planet_index = (0 + hour_index - 1) % 7  # 0=Sun in our sequence
        result["planet"] = chaldean_order[planet_index]
        result["hour_type"] = "day"  # Conceptually all day in polar day
        result["hour_index"] = hour_index
        
        # Calculate start/end for this hour
        hour_start = datetime.replace(hour=0, minute=0, second=0, microsecond=0) + timedelta(hours=(hour_index-1)*2)
        hour_end = hour_start + timedelta(hours=2)
        
        result["start_time"] = hour_start
        result["end_time"] = hour_end
        result["warnings"].append("Polar day conditions - using equal hours approximation")
        
        return result
    
    # Polar night conditions
    if temporal_hours["is_polar_night"]:
        # Using equal hours for polar night
        hours_since_midnight = (datetime - datetime.replace(hour=0, minute=0, second=0, microsecond=0)).total_seconds() / 3600.0
        hour_index = int((hours_since_midnight % 24) / 2) + 1
        
        # Determine planet (starting with Saturn at midnight for consistency)
        # In polar night, we start with Saturn as first night hour conceptually
        planet_index = (5 + hour_index - 1) % 7  # 5=Saturn in our sequence
        result["planet"] = chaldean_order[planet_index]
        result["hour_type"] = "night"  # Conceptually all night in polar night
        result["hour_index"] = hour_index
        
        # Calculate start/end for this hour
        hour_start = datetime.replace(hour=0, minute=0, second=0, microsecond=0) + timedelta(hours=(hour_index-1)*2)
        hour_end = hour_start + timedelta(hours=2)
        
        result["start_time"] = hour_start
        result["end_time"] = hour_end
        result["warnings"].append("Polar night conditions - using equal hours approximation")
        
        return result
    
    # Normal conditions - find which hour period contains the timestamp
    # Check day hours first
    for start, end, hour_index in temporal_hours["day_hours"]:
        # Sunrise is inclusive, sunset is exclusive for day hours
        if start <= datetime < end:
            result["hour_type"] = "day"
            result["hour_index"] = hour_index
            
            # Calculate planet ruler - advance from day ruler
            # For day hour n, planet index = (start_index + n - 1) % 7
            planet_index = (start_index + hour_index - 1) % 7
            result["planet"] = chaldean_order[planet_index]
            result["start_time"] = start
            result["end_time"] = end
            return result
    
    # Check night hours
    for start, end, hour_index in temporal_hours["night_hours"]:
        # Sunset is inclusive, next sunrise is exclusive for night hours
        if start <= datetime < end:
            result["hour_type"] = "night"
            result["hour_index"] = hour_index
            
            # Calculate planet ruler - advance from day ruler through all day hours
            # Total offset = day hours (12) + night hour index - 1
            total_offset = 12 + hour_index - 1
            planet_index = (start_index + total_offset) % 7
            result["planet"] = chaldean_order[planet_index]
            result["start_time"] = start
            result["end_time"] = end
            return result
    
    # This should never happen with valid inputs
    warnings.append("Timestamp outside expected range - defaulting to Sun")
    result["warnings"] = warnings
    result["planet"] = "Sun"
    result["hour_type"] = "unknown"
    result["hour_index"] = None
    result["start_time"] = None
    result["end_time"] = None
    
    return result
```

This perspective arises from the observation that the solar cycle—day transitioning to night transitioning to day—represents the most fundamental rhythm of human existence. The insight that twelve divisions accommodated the seasonal variations in day length without requiring recalibration each day demonstrates remarkable ancient mathematical sophistication[23]. ## Comparative Analysis of Historical Sources

### Vettius Valens vs. William Lilly: Different Conventions

The historical record reveals two primary conventions for determining planetary hours, represented by key sources:

**Vettius Valens (2nd century CE)**:
- Used sunset as the day beginning[2]
- Defined hours as: "the hour I of night [is that] of Mercury; the second, [that] of [the] Moon; the third, [that] of Saturn"[11]
- Organized calculations around the night portion first
- Reflected Greek and Babylonian tradition[2]

**William Lilly (17th century CE)**:
- Used sunrise as the day beginning[1]
- Defined hours as: "the period that extends from sunrise to sunset (daylight) is divided into twelve hours"[1]
- Organized calculations around the day portion first
- Represented the dominant European tradition by the 17th century[7]

These differences reflect broader cultural transitions in timekeeping philosophy. The sunset convention aligned with agricultural societies where the day's work ended at sunset, while the sunrise convention aligned with societies where daily activity began at first light.

Our implementation adopts the sunrise convention (Lilly's approach) as the default standard for several reasons:
1. It aligns with the predominant modern astrological practice
2. The sunrise convention creates a more intuitive day progression (beginning with light)
3. It matches the Egyptian tradition, which had significant influence on Greek astronomy
4. The majority of contemporary resources use the sunrise convention

However, it's important to recognize that historically both conventions were valid and widely used. The Pompeian graffito evidence confirms that the sunset convention was in use in the 1st century CE[2], and the sunrise convention appears in Medieval manuscripts.

### Chaldean Order Documentation

The Chaldean order sequence (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon) is remarkably consistent across historical sources:

- **William Lilly**: Clearly states the order in *Christian Astrology*: "Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sol, Venus, Mercury, Luna"[7]

The consistency of this sequence across cultures and centuries—despite the political and religious transformations that separated these civilizations—is remarkable. The mathematical explanation for the planetary day sequence—24 hours divided by 7 planets leaves a remainder of 3, thus advancing three positions in the sequence each day—appears in multiple sources[2]. ## Astronomical and Geographic Variations

### Latitude Effects on Temporal Hours

The duration of temporal hours varies significantly with latitude, creating different experiences of planetary hours across the globe. At the equator, day and night remain relatively balanced year-round, with temporal hours varying only slightly in duration[23].

As one moves toward the poles, the seasonal variations in day length become more extreme:
- At 49° north/south latitude (e.g., London), day length varies between approximately 16 equinoctial hours in summer and 8 equinoctial hours in winter[23]
- At 60° north/south latitude, summer days can exceed 19 equinoctial hours while winter days can be less than 5 hours
- Within the polar circles (66.5°), polar day and polar night create extended periods of continuous daylight or darkness

These variations dramatically affect the temporal hours:
- At high latitudes, summer day hours can be significantly longer than night hours
- At high latitudes, winter night hours can be significantly longer than day hours
- At extreme latitudes, the standard calculation becomes impossible during polar day/night

The planetary hours system thus creates a highly localized time experience—two people in different latitudes experience the same "day" differently in terms of hour durations, even if they share the same planetary day ruler.

### Seasonal Variations

The seasonal variations in daylight and darkness create predictable patterns in temporal hour durations:
- At equinoxes: Day and night hours are equal (60 minutes each)
- Summer solstice: Day hours are longest, night hours shortest (in Northern Hemisphere)
- Winter solstice: Night hours are longest, day hours shortest (in Northern Hemisphere)

These variations follow a sinusoidal pattern throughout the year, with the rate of change most rapid around the equinoxes and slowest around the solstices[23]. This creates a continuous flow of temporal hour durations that ancient practitioners would have experienced intuitively.

The recognition of these patterns appears in historical sources. William Lilly notes: "Astrologians do not so, but follow this method, viz. according to the motion of the Sun both Summer and Winter, so do they vary their hours in length or shortness"[21].

This seasonal awareness connects the planetary hours system to broader agricultural and cultural practices that aligned with the solar cycle—a connection that modern mechanical timekeeping has largely severed.

## Modern Adaptations and Challenges

### Polar Regions and the Planetary Hours System

The standard planetary hours system encounters fundamental challenges at high latitudes where the sun does not rise or set for extended periods[16]. These challenges represent genuine limitations of the traditional system, as ancient practitioners never needed to address polar conditions.

At approximately 66.5° latitude and beyond (the polar circles), the sun fails to set every day in summer and fails to rise every day in winter[16]. At the poles themselves, these conditions extend for approximately six months each—polar day followed by polar night.

The traditional method of dividing the solar cycle into day and night hours becomes impossible under these conditions because either the day period (sunrise to sunset) or night period (sunset to sunrise) has zero duration, making division by 12 mathematically undefined.

Our implementation handles these cases with a modern adaptation:
1. For polar day (continuous daylight): Use 24 equal hours (60 minutes)
2. For polar night (continuous darkness): Use 24 equal hours (60 minutes)
3. For both cases: Apply the standard planetary sequence starting with the appropriate day/night ruler

This approach represents a practical modern solution, though it's important to acknowledge that it's an adaptation not found in historical sources. The equal hours approximation preserves the planetary sequence while acknowledging the physical impossibility of dividing time into hours of zero duration.

### Daylight Saving Time Complications

Daylight Saving Time (DST) creates significant complications for planetary hours calculation:
- Creates a 1-hour discontinuity twice yearly
- Shifts the relationship between clock time and solar time
- Creates ambiguity in timestamp interpretation

The fundamental problem is that planetary hours follow the natural solar cycle while DST creates artificial clock shifts that disconnect from solar time. For example, when DST begins, clocks jump forward one hour while the solar cycle continues uninterrupted—creating a missing hour in the clock time that doesn't correspond to any solar time.

The proper implementation approach is:
1. Convert all timestamps to local standard time (without DST)
2. Calculate planetary hours using standard time
3. Convert results back to local time (with DST) for display
4. For timestamps during the DST transition hour, apply appropriate adjustments

This ensures that planetary hours always align with the actual solar cycle rather than the artificial clock shifts of DST.
