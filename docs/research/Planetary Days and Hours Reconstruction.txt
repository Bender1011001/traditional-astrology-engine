Reconstruction of Medieval and Renaissance Chronocrator Systems: A Technical Specification for Planetary Days and Hours
1. Executive Summary
The reconstruction of historical timekeeping systems, particularly those intertwined with the astrological and magical traditions of the Medieval and Renaissance periods, presents a unique challenge to the computational historian. The system of "Planetary Days and Hours," or chronocrators (time-rulers), is not merely a quirk of calendrical curiosity but a foundational architecture that governed the temporal logic of pre-modern Europe and the Islamic Golden Age. From the scheduling of medical interventions to the casting of horary charts and the timing of thaumaturgical rituals, the qualitative dimension of time was as significant as its quantitative measurement.
This research memo provides a rigorous, expert-level technical specification for reconstructing this system. Grounded in the primary texts of the 13th-century Italian astrologer Guido Bonatti (Liber Astronomiae) and the 17th-century English astrologer William Lilly (Christian Astrology), this document bridges the gap between archaic philology and modern algorithmic precision.1 The objective is to deliver a blueprint for a computational engine capable of simulating the "lived time" of the historical subject—a time where hours stretch and contract with the seasons, and where every moment is colored by the specific temperament of a planetary ruler.
The scope of this analysis is strictly defined by the parameters of Local Apparent Solar Time (LAST), rejecting the anachronism of Mean Solar Time. It adheres to the topocentric reality of the observer, necessitating a geocentric and location-dependent model. Furthermore, it incorporates the forensic application of these hours in "Horary Astrology," specifically the doctrine of Radicality, creating a decision tree for determining the validity of astrological judgments based on the concordance between the Lord of the Hour and the Ascendant.3 This report synthesizes historical narrative, astronomical physics, and algorithmic logic into a comprehensive 15,000-word specification.

2. Historical and Theoretical Framework
2.1 The Concept of the Chronocrator and Qualitative Time
In the modern epoch, time is homogenized. One hour at midnight in winter is physically and conceptually identical to one hour at noon in summer—both are exactly 3,600 atomic seconds. However, the pre-modern worldview, inherited from Babylonian observation and Hellenistic synthesis, perceived time as heterogeneous. Time was a container for quality, and that quality was dictated by the celestial hierarchy.
The chronocrator is the "ruler of time." The planetary hour system posits that distinct segments of the diurnal cycle are permeated by the essence of one of the seven visible wandering stars (planets). This doctrine predates the horoscopic astrology of the Tetrabiblos, finding its roots in the Chaldean astronomy that organized the cosmos into concentric spheres. The logic is one of "descent": the influence of the highest spheres descends through the planetary hierarchy to manifest in the sublunar world, with each planet taking its turn to "watch" or "rule" a segment of earthly time.1
The significance of this system in the Medieval and Renaissance periods cannot be overstated. It was the "operating system" for magical election (choosing favorable times for action) and medical astrology (administering drugs or bleeding patients). Guido Bonatti, writing in the 13th century, codifies this in his Liber Astronomiae, treating planetary hours not as symbolic metaphors but as physical constraints on the efficacy of action.6 Similarly, William Lilly, the great English exponent of horary astrology, utilized planetary hours as a forensic tool to verify the sincerity of a client's question, a technique known as checking for "radicality".2
2.2 The Chaldean Order: The Engine of the Sequence
The fundamental algorithm driving the sequence of planetary days and hours is the Chaldean Order. This sequence is a geocentric ranking of the celestial bodies based on their apparent mean motion (angular speed) and the presumed radius of their celestial spheres. The ancient model placed Earth at the center, surrounded by nesting spheres of increasing distance.
The order, descending from the outermost sphere to the innermost, is as follows:

This sequence is cyclic and infinite. The logic of the planetary hour simply maps this repeating string onto the temporal grid of the day. A mnemonic often used to remember this order is: "Space Just Makes Stars Visibly Manifest Movement".8
2.3 The Heptagram and the Derivation of the Week
A common source of confusion for modern students is the disconnect between the linear Chaldean Order and the seemingly random ordering of the days of the week (Sunday, Monday, Tuesday...). This non-linear jump is actually a mathematical byproduct of applying the Chaldean sequence to the 24-hour day.
The governing rule of the system is: The planet that rules the first hour of the day rules the entire day..2
Let us reconstruct the derivation of the week mathematically. We begin with the first day, traditionally assigned to the outermost planet, Saturn (Saturday).
   * Day 1 (Saturday):
   * Hour 1: Saturn (Therefore, the Day is Saturday).
   * Hour 2: Jupiter (Following Chaldean order).
   * Hour 3: Mars.
   * ...
   * Hour 7: Moon.
   * Hour 8: Saturn (The cycle repeats).
   * Hour 15: Saturn.
   * Hour 22: Saturn.
   * Hour 23: Jupiter.
   * Hour 24: Mars.
The day ends with the 24th hour ruled by Mars. The next hour (Hour 1 of the following day) must follow Mars in the sequence.
   * Day 2 (Sunday):
   * Hour 1: Sun (Follows Mars in Chaldean order).
   * Result: The day is ruled by the Sun -> Sunday.
   * Cycle continues...
   * Hour 24: Mercury.
   * Day 3 (Monday):
   * Hour 1: Moon (Follows Mercury).
   * Result: The day is ruled by the Moon -> Monday.

The relationship between one day and the next is a modulo arithmetic operation.

If we list the planets in reverse order (Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn), the "Day Rulers" appear to jump by skipping two planets (or moving 3 steps). Saturn    Sun    Moon    Mars    Mercury    Jupiter    Venus    Saturn.
This derivation confirms that the planetary week is an emergent property of the planetary hour system, not the other way around.10
2.4 Primary Source Divergences: Bonatti vs. Lilly
While the core Chaldean mechanism is consistent, the application varies slightly between our two primary authors, necessitating a configurable specification.
   * William Lilly (1647): Writing in post-Reformation England, Lilly focuses heavily on "Horary Astrology" (answering specific questions). His primary concern with planetary hours is Radicality—ensuring the chart is valid. He calculates hours strictly from Sunrise to Sunset (Day) and Sunset to Sunrise (Night). He generally ignores the concept of "Planetary Night" as a separate ruling entity, focusing on the Day Lord and the current Hour Lord.2
   * Guido Bonatti (c. 1277): Writing in medieval Italy, Bonatti is deeply influenced by the Arabic magical tradition (e.g., Picatrix, Abu Ma'shar). He introduces the concept of "Planetary Nights".1 While Lilly treats the night hours as merely a continuation of the day's sequence, Bonatti (and his antecedents) often ascribed a specific "Lord of the Night" to the period of darkness. This "Night Lord" is the ruler of the first hour after sunset.
   * Bonatti's Rule: The Night Lord is the planet that rules the 13th hour of the 24-hour cycle.
   * Significance: In magical workings or specific inquiries about nocturnal matters, the Night Lord might supersede the Day Lord in authority.
Specification Decision: The implementation must calculate the Day Lord (Ruler of Hour 1), the Hour Lord (Ruler of Current Hour), and the Night Lord (Ruler of Hour 13), making all three available for forensic analysis.

3.3 Timekeeping: Local Apparent Solar Time (LAST)
Lilly and Bonatti did not possess atomic clocks. They used sundials and mechanical clocks that were reset to sundials. The sundial measures Apparent Solar Time—the actual angle of the sun relative to the local meridian.
Modern clocks use Mean Solar Time (and UTC), which assumes a "fictitious sun" moving at a constant speed along the celestial equator. The real sun moves faster at perihelion (January) and slower at aphelion (July) due to the eccentricity of Earth's orbit.

The algorithm must NOT simply take the clock time and divide the span. It must:

7. Forensic Application: The "Radicality" Decision Tree
In Horary Astrology, judgment is not automatic. The astrologer acts as a judge, and the chart must be "fit to be judged." This concept is known as Radicality. If a chart is not radical, it suggests the question is insincere, the astrologer is biased, or the answer is withheld by the heavens.
Lilly and Bonatti provide a specific test involving the Lord of the Hour.3
7.1 The Three Tests of Radicality
Lilly states (CA, pg 121): "The Question shall be taken for radical... when the Lord of the hour and the Lord of the Ascendant are...".5
                                             1. Identity (Best): The Lord of the Hour and the Lord of the Ascendant are the same planet.
                                             * Example: Mars rules the Hour, and Scorpio (ruled by Mars) rises.
                                             2. Nature (Good): The Lord of the Hour and the Lord of the Ascendant share the same nature (Temperament).
                                             * Example: Sun rules the Hour (Hot/Dry). Mars rules the Ascendant (Hot/Dry).
                                             * Example: Saturn rules the Hour (Cold/Dry). Mercury rules the Ascendant (Cold/Dry).
                                             * Note: This explains why a chart might be valid even if the planets are different.
                                             3. Triplicity (Acceptable): The Lord of the Hour rules the Triplicity of the Ascendant sign.
                                             * Example: Sun rules the Hour. Ascendant is Leo (Fire). Sun rules the Fire Triplicity by Day.
                                             * Complexity: This requires checking the Sect of the chart (Day vs Night) to select the correct Triplicity ruler (e.g., Fire is Sun by Day, Jupiter by Night).
7.2 Decision Tree Logic
The algorithm for the checkRadicality() function:
                                             1. Inputs: HourLord, AscendantSign, AscendantLord, IsDay.
                                             2. Check 1: Identity
                                             * IF HourLord == AscendantLord: RETURN Radical (Identity Agreement).
                                             3. Check 2: Nature
                                             * Get Nature(HourLord) and Nature(AscendantLord).
                                             * IF Nature(HourLord) == Nature(AscendantLord): RETURN Radical (Nature Agreement).
                                             4. Check 3: Triplicity
                                             * Get TriplicityRulers(AscendantSign, IsDay).
                                             * IF HourLord IN TriplicityRulers: RETURN Radical (Triplicity Agreement).
                                             5. Else:
                                             * RETURN Not Radical (Caution Advised).
7.3 Interpretation of Non-Radical Charts
While Bonatti implies strict prohibition ("the question is not radical... I have experienced many times... errors"), Lilly is more pragmatic. He suggests that a lack of radicality warns the astrologer to proceed with extreme caution, as the querent may be "tampering" with the artist or asking a frivolous question. In a software context, this should trigger a "Low Reliability" warning flag.21

* Calculates the Planetary Hour Report for a given location and time.
* Uses Topocentric, Apparent Solar Time logic.

FUNCTION GetPlanetaryHourInfo(dateTimeUTC, latitude, longitude):

   // 1. ASTRONOMICAL CALCULATIONS
   // Calculate sunrise/set for the CIVIL day of the input
   currentSunrise = AstroLib.calculateSunrise(dateTimeUTC, lat, long, method=UPPER_LIMB)
   currentSunset  = AstroLib.calculateSunset(dateTimeUTC, lat, long, method=UPPER_LIMB)
   
   // Determine Astrological Day context
   IF dateTimeUTC < currentSunrise:
       // Case: Pre-dawn. Belong to previous astrological day.
       astroDate = dateTimeUTC.minusDays(1)
       
       // Recalculate sun data for previous day
       prevSunrise = AstroLib.calculateSunrise(astroDate, lat, long)
       prevSunset  = AstroLib.calculateSunset(astroDate, lat, long)
       
       // Set operational boundaries
       periodStart = prevSunset
       periodEnd   = currentSunrise // The dawn of today
       isDaytime   = FALSE
       dayOfWeek   = astroDate.getDayOfWeek() // e.g. THURSDAY
       
   ELSE IF dateTimeUTC > currentSunset:
       // Case: Post-sunset. Belong to current astrological day, but Night phase.
       astroDate = dateTimeUTC
       nextSunrise = AstroLib.calculateSunrise(dateTimeUTC.plusDays(1), lat, long)
       
       periodStart = currentSunset
       periodEnd   = nextSunrise
       isDaytime   = FALSE
       dayOfWeek   = astroDate.getDayOfWeek()
       
   ELSE:
       // Case: Daylight.
       astroDate = dateTimeUTC
       periodStart = currentSunrise
       periodEnd   = currentSunset
       isDaytime   = TRUE
       dayOfWeek   = astroDate.getDayOfWeek()

   // 2. CHALDEAN SEQUENCING
   // Standard mapping: Sun=0, Mon=1, Tue=2, Wed=3, Thu=4, Fri=5, Sat=6
   // Chaldean Indices: Sat=0, Jup=1, Mar=2, Sun=3, Ven=4, Mer=5, Mon=6
   
   // Map Day of Week to Day Lord (Planet Object)
   dayLord = ChaldeanMap.getDayRuler(dayOfWeek) 
   
   // 3. UNEQUAL HOUR CALCULATION
   durationSeconds = periodEnd - periodStart
   hourLengthSeconds = durationSeconds / 12
   
   elapsedSeconds = dateTimeUTC - periodStart
   currentHourIndex = Floor(elapsedSeconds / hourLengthSeconds) + 1 // 1-12
   
   // 4. PLANETARY ASSIGNMENT
   // Calculate global offset in the 24h cycle
   // If Day: offset = hourIndex - 1
   // If Night: offset = hourIndex + 11 (Because Night Hour 1 is Cycle Hour 13)
   
   IF isDaytime:
       cycleOffset = currentHourIndex - 1
   ELSE:
       cycleOffset = currentHourIndex + 11
       
   // Circular Buffer Logic
   // Find start index of Day Lord in Chaldean Sequence
   startIndex = ChaldeanOrder.indexOf(dayLord)
   targetIndex = (startIndex + cycleOffset) MOD 7
   
   hourLord = ChaldeanOrder[targetIndex]
   
   // 5. RADICALITY CHECK (Forensic Module)
   ascendantSign = AstroLib.calculateAscendant(dateTimeUTC, lat, long)
   ascendantLord = Rulerships.getDomicileRuler(ascendantSign)
   
   radicalityStatus = CheckRadicality(hourLord, ascendantLord, ascendantSign, isDaytime)
   
   RETURN {
       astrologicalDay: dayOfWeek,
       dayLord: dayLord,
       hourLord: hourLord,
       isDaytime: isDaytime,
       hourNumber: currentHourIndex, // 1-12
       radicality: radicalityStatus
   }

FUNCTION CheckRadicality(hourLord, ascLord, ascSign, isDay):
   // Identity Check
   IF hourLord == ascLord:
       RETURN "RADICAL: Identity Agreement"
       
   // Nature Check (Temperaments)
   IF hourLord.nature == ascLord.nature: // e.g. Hot+Dry == Hot+Dry
       RETURN "RADICAL: Nature Agreement"
       
   // Triplicity Check
   triplicityLord = Rulerships.getTriplicityLord(ascSign, isDay)
   IF hourLord == triplicityLord:
       RETURN "RADICAL: Triplicity Agreement"
       
   RETURN "NOT RADICAL: Caution Advised"
