The Mathematical and Geometrical Derivation of the Alcabitius House System: An Exhaustive Analysis of Spherical Mechanics and Temporal Projection
1. Introduction to the Alcabitius Paradigm in Spherical Astronomy
The division of the local sky into twelve sectors, known as "houses" in western horoscopic astrology, represents one of the most complex problems in historical spherical astronomy. Among the myriad systems developed over two millennia, the Alcabitius System (also known as the Alchabitius or "Standard System" of the Middle Ages) stands as a distinct architectural achievement in the geometry of time. Unlike space-based systems that slice the celestial sphere using static great circles—such as the Campanus system’s use of the Prime Vertical or the Regiomontanus system’s division of the Celestial Equator—the Alcabitius system is fundamentally dynamic. It anchors the division of the sky to the specific diurnal motion of the Ascendant, projecting the "rhythm" of the rising degree onto the rest of the ecliptic.
This report provides a rigorous, exhaustive derivation of the Alcabitius system. It treats the problem strictly as an exercise in spherical geometry and geocentric celestial mechanics. We eschew modern approximations in favor of first-principles derivations using the properties of the Celestial Sphere. The analysis covers the full mathematical pipeline: from the calculation of the primary angles (Ascendant and Midheaven) using true obliquity, through the derivation of the Ascendant’s semi-arc, to the trisection algorithms that define the intermediate cusps, and finally, the coordinate transformations required to project these temporal points onto the ecliptic.
Furthermore, we address the critical divergences in modern computational implementation, specifically the distinction between the historical "Standard" method (projected via Hour Circles) and the variant often labeled "Alcabitius Semi-Arc" (projected via Vertical Circles). This distinction is vital for software engineers and astronomical researchers seeking to replicate historical data or validate the system’s behavior in high-latitude zones where spherical distortions become extreme.
1.1 The Historical "Standard"
The system derives its name from the 10th-century Arab mathematician and astrologer Al-Qabisi (Latinized as Alcabitius), particularly from his work Introduction to the Art of Judgments of the Stars.1 However, the mathematical underpinnings predate Al-Qabisi, appearing in the works of 5th-century rhetorician Rhetorius the Egyptian and referenced in the Almagest lineage of Ptolemaic astronomy. Historian John D. North, in his seminal work Horoscopes and History, classifies this as the "Standard System" due to its overwhelming prevalence in medieval scientific manuscripts from the 10th to the 15th centuries.2
The endurance of the Alcabitius system during this period is not merely an artifact of tradition but a testament to its mathematical elegance. In an era before logarithmic tables or digital computers, the Alcabitius method offered a stable, calculable framework that linked the local horizon (via the Ascendant) to the meridian (the Midheaven) through a single unifying variable: the Diurnal Semi-Arc of the rising degree. By defining the intermediate house cusps as the points of the ecliptic that culminate at specific intervals of this semi-arc, the system creates a temporal symmetry that resonates with the primary motion of the sky.
1.2 The Geometric Premise
The fundamental question posed by the Alcabitius system is: If the current Ascendant were to continue its diurnal motion, how long would it take to reach the Midheaven?
This duration is the Diurnal Semi-Arc. The system trisects this duration to find two intermediate moments in time. The 11th and 12th house cusps are then defined as the degrees of the ecliptic that will be culminating at those respective moments. This is a "Time-Axis" system. It does not divide the ecliptic directly (like the Equal House system) nor does it divide the Prime Vertical (Campanus). It divides the Time it takes for the sector of the sky currently rising to reach its culmination.
Crucially, the standard Alcabitius method projects these time points onto the ecliptic using lines of Right Ascension (Hour Circles). This preserves the equatorial nature of the division, maintaining the link between the rotation of the Earth (Primary Motion) and the division of the chart.

2. Foundations of Spherical Geometry and Coordinate Systems

Since the distance to the stars/planets is effectively infinite for the geometric division of space, the distinction between topocentric and geocentric is negligible for the definition of the sectors themselves, though parallax applies to specific bodies. We proceed with the Geocentric model.

4. The Theory of Semi-Arcs
The central engine of the Alcabitius system is the Diurnal Semi-Arc (DSA).
4.1 Definition of the Semi-Arc
The Diurnal Arc of a celestial body is the portion of its parallel of declination that lies above the horizon.
The Diurnal Semi-Arc is half of this arc—specifically, the arc from the Eastern Horizon to the Upper Meridian (Culmination).
In the Alcabitius system, we are not interested in the semi-arc of the sun or a planet, but specifically the Semi-Arc of the Ascendant degree.
The system assumes that the "character" of the chart is imprinted by the rising degree. The "time structure" of the houses is determined by how long it takes that specific rising degree to reach the midheaven.
